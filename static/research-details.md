## 1. Registration Walls in Digital News: Privacy, Access, and Shifts in Attention

**Authors:** Donghwa Bae, Raveesh Mayya, Adithya Pattabhiramaiah, Tingting Nian
**Outlet:** MIS Quarterly, forthcoming 2026
**Stream:** Digital platform policy; privacy; first-party data

**Research question:** How do effort-based access barriers (registration walls, which require users to create accounts and share personal information for content access) affect user engagement with digital news, as the industry shifts from third-party to first-party data collection?

**Theory:** Two mechanisms are theorized: an effort-investment pathway, where intrinsically motivated readers tolerate added friction, and an alternative-option pathway, where users with lower match value at a given outlet switch away. Privacy salience may moderate these effects in either direction.

**Methodology and data:** Natural experiment around the New York Times' August 2019 registration wall implementation, analyzed with a unique consumer browsing panel dataset. Heterogeneity analyses split users into news-multihomers (consume news from multiple journalistic sources) and news-singlehomers (rely primarily on NYT). Regional exposure to documented privacy incidents is used to measure privacy salience.

**Findings:** Strong support for the effort-investment mechanism. News-multihomers show marginally increased engagement with NYT after the wall; news-singlehomers reduce engagement. Users in regions with higher exposure to privacy incidents respond less negatively, most pronounced among singlehomers. Users who reduce NYT engagement display distinct switching patterns influenced by prior engagement, demographics, and privacy attitudes.

## 2. Do Non-monetary Gifts Enhance or Diminish Voluntary Paid Gifts? Evidence from a Videogame Live-streaming Platform

**Authors:** Peiyan Yu, Raveesh Mayya, Anindya Ghose
**Outlet:** Information Systems Research, Articles in Advance. Link: https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2024.0902
**Stream:** Token policy; platform monetization design

**Research question:** Does introducing non-monetary gift channels cannibalize or complement voluntary monetary gift-giving in video-game live-streaming, where paid gifts within the broadcast window are streamers' dominant revenue source?

**Theory:** When platform design provides real-time public visibility, value-differentiated gift tiers, and synchronous interaction, non-monetary gifts can complement monetary gifts through costly signaling with norm elevation.

**Methodology and data:** Exogenous policy change on a major live-streaming platform that increased viewers' access to non-monetary gifts; individual-level transaction data; multiple modern causal inference approaches.

**Findings:** Non-monetary gifts significantly increase monetary gift spending. Primary driver is signaling dilution: non-monetary gifts replicate the reputation value of the lowest paid tier, prompting viewers to escalate to costlier options to maintain differentiation. Early evidence of habit formation: previously non-paying viewers who send non-monetary gifts are significantly more likely to begin and sustain monetary gifting. Net effect is complementarity, robust across viewer experience levels, streamer popularity tiers, and game genres.

## 3. Growing Platforms by Adding Complementors Without a Contract

**Authors:** Raveesh Mayya, Zhuoxin (Allen) Li
**Outlet:** Information Systems Research, 2025, 36(6), 1670-1690. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2023.0237
**Stream:** Platform design; platform growth strategy

**Research question:** What happens when a platform grows by listing non-partnered (non-contracted) suppliers, a strategy in which third-party enablers facilitate trade with businesses that have no formal agreement with the platform?

**Context and theory:** On-demand food delivery platforms (e.g., Grubhub) listed non-partnered restaurants, allowing consumers to order from them via third-party deliverers. Non-contracted partnerships can be advantageous when formal partnership costs are non-negligible and consumers value selection variety, but raise concerns about outdated menus, pricing errors, and poor ratings.

**Methodology and data:** Data from Grubhub; quasi-experimental analysis exploiting a California policy change that banned non-partnered listings (regulatory delisting).

**Findings:** Being listed as non-partnered boosts takeout revenue for those restaurants, particularly independent ones, with positive spillovers to partnered restaurants. Regulatory delisting reverses these gains, highlighting the tension between platform growth strategies and regulatory actions.

## 4. Startup Accelerators, Information Asymmetry, and Corporate Venture Capital Investments

**Authors:** Raveesh Mayya, Peng Huang
**Outlet:** Management Science, 2025, 71(11), 9123-9144. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.03494
**Stream:** Information asymmetry; entrepreneurial ecosystems

**Research question:** Prior work shows accelerators help local economies and startups; this paper examines a third beneficiary: investors. How does a corporate venture capitalist's (CVC) investment pattern in a region shift following a startup accelerator's entry?

**Theory:** CVC investments are motivated by strategic objectives (early exposure to emerging technologies), but information asymmetry pushes CVCs toward startups with high business relatedness to the parent, which are less risky but lack knowledge novelty. Accelerators mitigate information asymmetry via two pathways: quality signals, and mentorship and training.

**Methodology and data:** Difference-in-differences style analyses of CVC investment patterns in regions before and after accelerator entry, focusing on the business relatedness between the CVC's parent corporation and portfolio companies.

**Findings:** CVCs increase investments in startups dissimilar to their parent's business after accelerator entry, i.e., they invest more diversely. The change is most pronounced for CVCs whose parent operates in an IT-using (rather than IT-producing) industry, suggesting accelerators help IT-using firms gain a foothold in the technology space.

## 5. Delaying Informed Consent: An Empirical Investigation of Mobile Apps' Upgrade Decisions

**Authors:** Raveesh Mayya, Siva Viswanathan
**Outlet:** Management Science, 2025, 71(8), 7113-7135. https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.00334
**Stream:** Privacy; platform policy implementation

**Research question:** When a platform grants complementors a flexible time window to adopt a privacy policy (rather than mandating uniform compliance), what are the consequences of delayed adoption, and who delays?

**Context:** The upgrade to Android 6.0, which gave consumers more control over personal data via runtime permissions. Apps had a three-year grace period during which non-adopting apps could still run on the latest Android version.

**Methodology and data:** Installed 13,691 popular apps on emulators to detect exactly when each app upgraded to Android 6.0; combined with multiple datasets on downloads, ratings, permissions, and in-app advertising to quantify the impact of delaying.

**Findings:** Delaying the upgrade results in significant losses of downloads and user ratings; frequent maintenance without upgrading only partially mitigates the decline. Apps that display in-app advertising and overreach for permissions are more likely to delay, suggesting a strategic trade-off between marketplace outcomes and continuous data collection. Design of policy implementation should anticipate participants' strategic behavior.

## 6. Who Forgoes Screening in Online Markets and Why? Evidence from Airbnb

**Authors:** Raveesh Mayya, Shun Ye, Siva Viswanathan, Rajshree Agarwal
**Outlet:** MIS Quarterly, 2021, 45(4), 1745-1776. https://doi.org/10.25300/misq/2021/15335
**Stream:** Platform design; screening and discrimination in peer-to-peer markets

**Research question:** Screening alleviates information asymmetry in peer-to-peer platforms but has raised discrimination concerns. Why do some providers voluntarily forgo screening, and with what performance consequences?

**Context:** Airbnb's "Instant Book" feature, which lets hosts forgo screening guests.

**Methodology and data:** Unique panel dataset of all Airbnb listings in New York City, August 2015 to February 2017. First explores antecedents of voluntarily switching to Instant Book, then causally identifies the impacts of switching.

**Findings:** Forgoing screening yields economic benefits through increased occupancy even as review ratings decline; effects are stronger for Black and female hosts. The paper discusses strategic and social-welfare implications for conversations on discrimination and bias in the sharing economy.
